\chapter{Data Center Growth in California}
\label{ch:growth}

This chapter answers RQ1: how California's planned data center capacity varies by stage of development, in absolute terms and relative to the scale of the existing grid. It first reproduces the national construction-spending series that frames public discussion, tests it for structural breaks, and then asks whether California's own indicators show the same acceleration. It ends with every vintage of the CEC's energization tiers and the RQ1 table. All code is in \texttt{src/ch2\_growth.py} and \texttt{notebooks/02\_data\_center\_growth.ipynb}.

\section{The national construction boom, reproduced}
\label{sec:census}

The Census Bureau's Value of Construction Put in Place survey has carried a ``data center'' line under private nonresidential construction since January 2014 \citep{census_c30}. Figure~\ref{fig:census} reproduces the widely circulated chart from the raw seasonally adjusted workbook and adds month-over-month growth. Spending at a seasonally adjusted annual rate rose from \$1.6~billion in January 2014 to \$13.9~billion in November 2022 and \$75.2~billion in July 2026 (preliminary). Data centers were half a percent of private nonresidential construction in 2014, two percent when ChatGPT launched, and ten percent in July 2026. Month-over-month growth averaged 4.1~percent from 2023 through mid-2026 against 1.2~percent in 2019 through 2021. No comparable state-level series exists: the Census publishes this category for the nation only, and the California picture must be built from proxies (Section~\ref{sec:proxies}).

\section{Structural breaks and forecasts}
\label{sec:breaks}

The analysis works on the log of the seasonally adjusted series, so that a linear trend is a constant compound growth rate. Three questions are asked in turn: does a break at the known date change the trend (Chow test, \citealp{chow1960}); where does the data itself place breaks when none is imposed (Bai-Perron, \citealp{baiperron1998,baiperron2003}); and what does a time-series model project. Growth rates carry Newey-West standard errors. The Bai-Perron search uses 15~percent trimming (a minimum segment of 23 months) and allows up to five breaks, the most the trimming permits, so that the choice of $m$ is never censored by the search. The number of breaks is chosen three ways: by BIC, by the LWZ criterion, and by the sequential supF($l+1|l$) procedure, in which each additional break is tested against the $l$-break model with a moving-block residual bootstrap $p$-value. supF(0$|$1) gets the same bootstrap under the no-break null, and the single-break date a residual-bootstrap 90~percent interval. The \texttt{ruptures} dynamic-programming search \citep{truong2020} is run as an independent check.

The known-date test is unambiguous. A break in intercept and slope at December 2022, the first full month after the November~30 launch, gives a Chow $F$ of 63 ($p < 0.001$) and the HAC-robust Wald test agrees ($p < 0.001$). Compound growth was 26~percent a year (95~percent interval 22 to 31) through November 2022 and 53~percent a year (46 to 61) from December 2022. Neither the date annotation nor the test establishes that the launch caused the acceleration; they establish that the trend changed then. Letting the data locate the breaks changes the story in two respects (Tables~\ref{tab:bp} and \ref{tab:seg}). BIC and LWZ both choose four breaks, in October 2016, December 2018, March 2022 and June 2024. The current expansion therefore began eight months before ChatGPT, in the hyperscaler capital-spending ramp of early 2022, and it has already slowed once: the segment from March 2022 to May 2024 grew at 61~percent a year (56 to 67) and the segment from June 2024 to July 2026 at 36~percent a year (31 to 41), still four times the 9~percent of 2019 to early 2022. The sequential procedure is more conservative. supF(1$|$0) of 125 rejects the no-break model ($p = 0.02$), but supF(2$|$1) of 37 has a bootstrap $p$-value of 0.085, so at the 5~percent level the procedure stops at one break, in November 2020, even though the third and fourth breaks are each significant once the earlier ones are in place ($p = 0.02$ and $p = 0.005$). \texttt{ruptures} reproduces all four BIC dates within one month. The forecasts in Table~\ref{tab:fc} continue the recent trend: an ARIMA(1,1,1) with drift and a damped-trend ETS, both fitted to log spending, put July 2027 near \$95 to \$100~billion and July 2028 near \$115 to \$135~billion, with 95~percent bands from roughly \$50 to \$260~billion by then. The point forecasts are back-transformed log medians, not arithmetic means, and the series is national nominal dollars, so the forecasts say nothing about California megawatts. A rolling-origin backtest over the last 24 months (Table~\ref{tab:bt}) shows why they are exploratory: at a twelve-month horizon the mean absolute percentage error is 14~percent for the ARIMA and 19~percent for the ETS against 13~percent for a log random walk with drift, every model ran below the outcomes on average, and the 95~percent bands covered 92~percent of outcomes for the ARIMA and 79~percent for the ETS. The bands, not the point forecasts, are the finding: a series whose growth rate has shifted four times in twelve years, and which a drift benchmark forecasts as well as either model, is not something a planner should extrapolate.

\paragraph{Employment and establishments.} The Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages counts private establishments and jobs in NAICS 518210, computing infrastructure, data processing, hosting and related services, by quarter \citep{qcew}. These are labour indicators of an industry, not counts of buildings or megawatts, and the industry includes hosting and processing firms that do not operate data centers. California employment in the industry rose from 25,000 in 2014 Q1 to 74,000 by the end of 2022, then flattened: 84,000 in 2026 Q1 after a plateau through 2024 and 2025. The Chow test at 2023 Q1 rejects a common trend ($F = 15$, $p < 0.001$), but the break is a deceleration, from 12~percent a year to roughly zero. Establishments show the same shape, 11~percent a year to 0.5~percent ($F = 11$). Data-located breaks in these smooth quarterly series are diffuse: BIC places four in each, the last in 2023 Q3 for employment and 2023 Q2 for establishments, but the single-break bootstrap is significant only for employment ($p = 0.035$ against $0.285$ for establishments) and the sequential procedure keeps no break in either. Wages decelerated from 22 to 11~percent a year ($p = 0.007$) with breaks in 2019 Q1 and 2021 Q4 that reflect the industry's earlier expansion. The county detail is in the notebook; Santa Clara, Los Angeles and San Francisco account for most of the state total.

\paragraph{MW under construction.} CBRE's semiannual market chapters give Silicon Valley colocation capacity under construction from H1 2016 and inventory from H1 2023 \citep{cbre_series}. Construction rose from 44~MW in H1 2016 to 142~MW by H2 2022 and has since ranged between 125 and 168~MW, with inventory creeping from 411 to 509~MW. Megawatts under construction are a stock whose change reflects completions as well as starts, and the series covers one colocation market, not California construction spending. The Chow test finds no break at H1 2023 ($p = 0.61$). BIC keeps a single break in H2 2020, when the pipeline stepped up from tens of megawatts to over a hundred, and growth has been 4~percent a year since; LWZ and the sequential procedure keep no break at all (bootstrap $p = 0.14$), which is what a plateau looks like in 21 semiannual points. Cushman \& Wakefield's tables are form-gated and JLL's report gives a single-period inventory, so the digitized series rests on CBRE; Southern California is available only from H1 2024 and is reported in the notebook.

\paragraph{Frontier facilities.} Epoch AI's hub tracks 87 large AI data centers from satellite imagery and permits, 75 of them in the United States, with per-site power timelines \citep{epoch_timelines}. Cumulative US power in these sites went from under 0.5~GW at the end of 2023 to 15.3~GW by September 2026, with a break in January 2023; the curve is a reconstruction from sampled sites, and its breaks can reflect the hub's coverage and retrospective estimates as much as construction. None of the 87 sites in the snapshot is in California. That is a limit of the hub's coverage, which Epoch itself describes as incomplete and uneven, not evidence that no frontier facility exists or is being built in California; no California series is drawn and no test is possible.

The three proxies agree on a finding that the national series hides: after 2022 California's data center industry did not accelerate in jobs, establishments or built Silicon Valley colocation capacity, and the largest AI campuses in Epoch's sample were sited elsewhere. The growth that California records is in requests to connect, which is the subject of the next section, and the divergence between requests and built capacity is exactly what the tiers are meant to measure.

\section{Every vintage of the energization tiers}
\label{sec:vintages}

Table~\ref{tab:vint} and Figure~\ref{fig:vint} assemble every published vintage of the CEC's data center energization requests, followed by the two utility-level series that carry the record past December 2025. The December 2024 data behind the 2024 IEPR Update are known only as PG\&E's and SCE's agreements plus applications, 5,808 and 963~MW, because inquiries were not published \citep{cec_prelim2025}. The summer 2025 data behind the preliminary 2025 forecast are published as totals by utility, 21,756~MW across seven utilities; for PG\&E and SCE the deck also gives agreements plus applications, 10,080 and 143~MW, so their inquiries were 1,588 and 5,685~MW \citep{cec_prelim2025}. The copy of that deck filed for the November 12, 2025 workshop labelled SCE at 2,492~MW and SVP at 1,382~MW; the published deck corrects these to 143 and 1,375~MW, and the corrected figures are used here. The December 2025 data behind the adopted forecast are the first complete vintage: 5,086~MW signed, 9,587~MW in application and 8,604~MW in inquiry, 23,277~MW in all, with PG\&E accounting for 14,747~MW and SCE for 4,624~MW by the memo's tier rows \citep{cec_dc_memo2026,cec_assembly2026}. The memo's footnote states that VEA's 2,600~MW of applications are for locations in Nevada, inside the CAISO footprint but outside California, and the CEC excludes them from statewide consumption; the pipeline located in California is therefore 20,677~MW (5,086 signed, 6,987 in application, 8,604 in inquiry), and the summer 2025 total contains the same 2,600~MW, leaving 19,156~MW in California. The published marginal totals and the utility rows differ by 1~MW (23,277 against 23,278); both are kept and the marginal totals are used. The August 2026 workshop restated the December 2025 figures and showed June 2026 known-load data only as charts \citep{cec_tn272026}. SCE's public project database provides the only vintages with cancellations: between its August 2025 and January 2026 versions, inquiries fell from 5,934 to 1,772~MW as about 3,100~MW moved into the application stage, canceled requests rose from 709 to 3,137~MW, and the active total fell from 6,201 to 5,162~MW \citep{sce_database2026}. The growth in SCE's total records between the two snapshots is cancellations, not pipeline; thirty-eight percent of everything SCE had ever logged was canceled within five months, the first direct California evidence on attrition. PG\&E's pipeline table from its second-quarter 2026 earnings, reproduced in its August 2026 presentation to the CEC, uses PG\&E's own stages and excludes inquiries: 5,090~MW in March 2026 and 12,710~MW in June 2026, of which 3,880~MW had a signed work performance agreement, 490~MW an interconnection construction agreement and 140~MW was in construction \citep{pge_dc_forecasting2026}. The jump of almost 8,000~MW in one quarter came from the 2026 cluster study. The Public Advocates Office observes that PG\&E had filed agreements covering only about 650~MW with the CPUC for approval \citep{cal_advocates2026}, so even the most committed tier has layers of certainty inside it. The three source families of Figure~\ref{fig:vint}, the CEC's statewide vintages, SCE's database and PG\&E's earnings pipeline, differ in utility coverage, stage definitions and dates, so their bar heights are not one growth series, and signed agreements are commitments to connect, not operating capacity.

Against CAISO's record instantaneous peak of 52,061~MW on September~6, 2022 \citep{caiso_keystats}, the California pipeline is 40~percent (40~percent against the hourly EIA-930 peak of 51,104~MW, and 45~percent for the reported total including Nevada). Against the roughly 1,000~MW of existing data center peak demand that the CEC reports for December 2025, it is 21 times; against the 800 to 1,065~MW of existing average load implied by the chapter~1 statewide estimate and assumed conversion, 19 to 26 times. Table~\ref{tab:rq1} answers RQ1. The denominators come from the sources of the preceding chapters: the CAISO record and 2025 peaks, the CEC's existing-load figure and the chapter~1 range, and the adopted 2025 IEPR planning forecast, whose hourly CAISO forecast gives a managed net peak of 46,479~MW in 2025 and 50,498~MW in 2030, a growth of 4,019~MW, and whose statewide energy to serve load grows from 263 to 298~TWh \citep{ced2025_tables}. Signed agreements alone, 5,086~MW, exceed the forecast's entire five-year peak growth (127~percent of it); the three California tiers together are 5.1 times that growth (5.8 times including the Nevada requests), 3.4 times it even at the CEC's 67~percent utilization factor, and their energy equivalent at that utilization and a 0.88 load factor, about 107~TWh a year, is 3.1 times the forecast's statewide energy growth to 2030. Requests are not simultaneous demand: signed agreements are not operating capacity, and the forecast's confidence and utilization factors exist to discount exactly this. The forecast is not blind to data centers: its planning scenario carries a data center contribution at the CAISO peak that rises from 96~MW in 2025 to 1,743~MW in 2030 (4,377~MW in the local-reliability scenario), and data-center-only deliveries of 12.1~TWh in 2030, roughly a third of projected statewide energy growth. What the forecast does is discount the requests, by the confidence levels and utilization of Chapter~\ref{ch:current}, to about a tenth of their face value at the peak by 2030. Whether that discount is right is the question the remaining chapters take up with the supply side, the attrition evidence and the adequacy framing.
