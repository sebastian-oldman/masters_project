\chapter{Discussion and Policy}
\label{ch:discussion}

\section{The contribution restated}
\label{sec:contribution}

This report is an independent replication and audit of the California Energy Commission's data center forecast method, carried out on public data with every assumption written down, varied and tested against outside evidence. It does not propose a competing forecast. It shows what the CEC's published parameters produce when applied to the CEC's published request data, where the result matches the Commission's own numbers and where it does not, what the same discipline does to the supply side, and how much of the resulting gap the assumptions that people argue about actually move.

Three results carry the argument. The CEC's confidence levels of 70/33/0 and 100/50/10 percent, its 67 percent utilization factor and the exemption of Silicon Valley Power's requests reproduce the Commission's 2040 endpoints of 4,855 and 7,381~MW to within 1~MW, and its ramp profile reproduces the 2030 components within 2 and 6 percent (Table~\ref{tab:cecrep}). The forecast is therefore transparent in the strict sense: anyone with the request table and the memo can rebuild it. Second, when the supply side is discounted with a completion model estimated on California's own planned generators, a third of the 2030 planned list disappears and the weighted fleet adds on average 5~TWh and 1.1~GW of ELCC by 2030 once retirements are netted (Table~\ref{tab:mcsummary}), so the gap that opens is mostly not a data center gap. Third, the proposal's upper bound of every requested megawatt operating flat is four to five times the probability-weighted 95th percentile, which is the quantitative form of the point Manner made for Finland: the registry number and the load that will exist are different quantities, and the difference is the object of study rather than an error to be corrected.

\paragraph{Confidence levels.} The CEC's 70/33/0 percent for agreements, applications and inquiries sit above what ERCOT's realized phase-transition rates would give over the same 60 months (37, 6 and 2 percent, Table~\ref{tab:ercotchain}) and, for agreements, below the 100 percent that PJM's firm rule and the CEC's own Local Reliability scenario assign. The ERCOT rates were measured while its queue quadrupled, so they measure study throughput as much as project viability, but they are the only empirical phase-transition evidence in the public record, and the CEC's central values are not conservative against it. The sensitivity analysis shows that this matters less than it seems: the three tier probabilities together carry about 2 percent of the variance of the energy gap and 4 percent of the peak gap (Table~\ref{tab:sobol}).

\paragraph{Utilization.} The 67 percent factor is the upper end of what SVP observed among its existing customers, by the memo's own account. ERCOT's observed monthly peaks of approved loads run at 46 to 64 percent of approved capacity depending on the month and the definition (Table~\ref{tab:ercotrates}, Figure~\ref{fig:ercot}); PJM uses 70 percent unless a utility supports more. The CEC's value is in the middle of that evidence and is defensible.

\paragraph{Ramp.} The CEC's ramp puts 36 percent of the Planning forecast's full demand on line by 2030 and 60 percent of the Local Reliability scenario's (Table~\ref{tab:cecramp}). PJM's minimum 36-month ramp would put all of a December 2025 agreement on line by 2030. The ramp is the data center assumption with the largest effect on the peak gap (a 2.2~GW swing between its 5th and 95th percentiles, Table~\ref{tab:tornado}), which argues for publishing the utility schedules it rests on.

\paragraph{The SVP exemption and the Nevada request.} Two features of the CEC forecast are not visible in its headline numbers. SVP's 1,038~MW of requests enter at full confidence, which is 350~MW of the Planning forecast's 2040 endpoint. VEA's 2,600~MW application is in Nevada but inside the CAISO balancing area, so it appears in the CAISO data center component (160 and 242~MW in 2030) and in the 23,277~MW statewide total. Both are legitimate choices; both should be stated where the totals are published.

\paragraph{The supply side.} The CEC discounts requests but the resource planning process does not discount planned generation in the same public way. California's own record says it should: 65 percent of the megawatts on the January planned lists of 2016 to 2022 were operating by December 2025, with gas at 44 percent and geothermal at 6 percent (Table~\ref{tab:realtech}), and the model-weighted 2030 list loses a third of its capacity. The realization model is the symmetric counterpart of the confidence levels and is the part of this audit most worth carrying into the state's own process.

\section{What the gap is, and what it is not}
\label{sec:gapmeaning}

The gap reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:together} is an increment: the growth of demand from 2025 to 2030 minus the growth of supply at recent operating patterns. Its median of 50~TWh and 7.2~GW is not a forecast of blackouts. It is the amount that has to come from somewhere other than the probability-weighted pipeline: from the existing gas fleet running harder (a capacity factor of 0.40 rather than 0.24 would close the median energy gap), from imports that have fallen from 103 to 62~TWh since 2012 and could rise again, from Diablo Canyon operating past 2030, from load flexibility, or from resources that are not yet on the planned list. The 2025 system already meets its peak with in-state resources plus imports, and the incremental gap says that the same position in 2030 needs 4 to 10~GW more than the weighted pipeline delivers. Data centers are about a third of that at the median and a smaller share of its uncertainty. The energy gap moves with imports, the non-data-center forecast, hydro and Diablo Canyon; the peak gap with Diablo Canyon, the forecast case, the ramp and generation completion (Figure~\ref{fig:sens}).

The upper bound is different in kind. Twenty gigawatts of flat load added to a 46~GW system is not a scenario the state's planning process can absorb, and no counting regime in the crosswalk treats it as one. Its value is as the ceiling that the confidence levels, utilization and ramp are discounting from, and the audit shows the discount to be a factor of thirteen in the CEC's central case.

\section{Policy implications}
\label{sec:policy}

\paragraph{For the CEC: publish the request data at the level the audit needed.} Everything in Section~\ref{sec:assumptions} was possible only because the memo states its parameters and SCE chose to publish its project-level database. The rest of the tiers are aggregates by utility. Publishing every request with its size class, requested energization year, tier and status history, as SCE already does and as ERCOT does in aggregate, would let the confidence levels be estimated from California's own attrition rather than asserted, would make the size thresholds of Texas and FERC applicable (in SCE's database 76 percent of active megawatts are in projects of 75~MW or more), and would make duplicate requests across utilities detectable.

\paragraph{For the CEC: report monthly and by phase.} ERCOT scores highest on the crosswalk because it publishes its queue every month by phase, and that series is what made the stock-flow case possible (Figure~\ref{fig:ercot}). An annual vintage cannot distinguish a project that advanced from one that was replaced. A monthly or quarterly publication of the tiers with the common taxonomy of Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} would cost little and would let the state estimate its own transition rates within two years.

\paragraph{For the CEC and the CPUC: separate firm from non-firm and say so.} PJM's rule, that only projects with a service obligation or construction commitment count in the near term, and the Texas forecast rule, that only executed interconnection agreements enter the forecast after 2026, both give California 5,086~MW (Table~\ref{tab:headline}). The CEC's Planning forecast already effectively does this through the zero weight on inquiries and the third weight on applications, but it presents the result as a single number. Publishing the firm and non-firm components separately, with the discount applied to each, would make the forecast comparable with PJM's and would make the choice of confidence level visible.

\paragraph{For the CPUC and CAISO: offer the flexible service the headroom pays for.} The replicated headroom of 3.1 to 4.6~GW at 0.25 to 1 percent curtailment (Table~\ref{tab:headroom}) is larger than every probability-weighted data center case and about equal to PJM's firm-only case, and almost all of the curtailment falls in a few hundred winter evening hours. FERC's June 2026 orders ask CAISO to propose new transmission services for flexible large loads \citep{ferc_showcause2026}; the headroom numbers are the evidence for what such a service could offer in California, and the negative-price hours and 3.8~TWh of curtailed wind and solar are the energy the same load could absorb.

\paragraph{For the resource planning process: weight the supply side.} A completion probability by technology and construction status, estimated as in Chapter~\ref{ch:supply} and updated each year from EIA-860M, should sit next to the confidence levels in the same forecast set. The two pumped-storage projects on the 2030 list, whose approvals have not been initiated, are the clearest case: 1,600~MW of nameplate that the model weights at 13 percent.

\paragraph{For the Legislature: the Diablo Canyon decision is the largest single lever.} The plant's status moves the median energy gap by 18~TWh and the peak gap by 2.2~GW, more than any data center assumption. The federal licences are in hand and state law stops at 2030; the decision belongs to the Legislature and should be taken with the 2030 balance in view, not after it.

\paragraph{For the federal data effort.} EIA's pilot survey covers Texas, Washington and Northern Virginia and not California \citep{eia_pilot2026}. A California sample would replace the four-way range of existing use in Chapter~\ref{ch:current} (7.0 to 9.3~TWh, about 1,000~MW) with a measurement.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The boundaries do not all coincide. Load, prices and carbon are CAISO's; generation, capacity and the forecast are statewide; the incremental form of the gap cancels the level mismatch but not the geography, and the absolute peak balance leaves out imports' contribution at the peak. The ELCC values come from the CPUC's 2023 study and the hydro and firm derates are stated assumptions. The ERCOT hazards were estimated over ten to twelve months during a surge and the no-studies hazard is an upper bound. Several ERCOT and PJM values were transcribed from chart images and are labelled as such. The reserve margin is an assumed range with no raw file behind it. Data center energy in 2030 is the annual rate at the end-2030 level, which overstates calendar-year 2030 energy for a load still ramping. Data center load is treated as flat at the system peak, which the CEC's own profiles support at 85 to 90 percent. The headroom replication uses seven years of load rather than Duke's nine and a cleaning of its own, and the winter threshold decides most of the answer. No project-level California request data exist outside SCE, so the tier probabilities could not be estimated from California's own attrition. The CEC's utility-supplied ramp schedules are not public, which is why the replicated 2030 components differ from the published ones by 2 to 6 percent.

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:future}

The monthly snapshot job that has run since the start of the project will, by the end of 2027, hold enough vintages of the CEC docket, ERCOT's queue, PJM's forecast and EIA-860M to estimate California's own tier transition rates and to test the ERCOT-calibrated case against what ERCOT's Batch Zero actually energizes. Matching SCE's project-level database to Epoch AI's facility timelines would give the first California check of paper requests against visible construction. Hourly profiles of the existing data center load, which the CEC has from utility interval meters but has published only as ratios, would replace the flat-load assumption in both the gap and the headroom. And the 2026 IEPR, which proposes a longer milestone list from inquiry to ramp, will be the first vintage against which the taxonomy of Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} can be tested.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

California's data center pipeline is 20,677~MW in three tiers, 40 percent of the record CAISO peak and twenty times the load that exists (RQ1). Weighted by the CEC's own parameters, which this report reproduces, and set against a supply side weighted by California's own completion record, the 2030 gap between demand growth and supply growth is 50~TWh and 7.2~GW at the median, with data centers 18~TWh and 2.3~GW of it, against an upper bound of 202 to 242~TWh and 25 to 30~GW (RQ2). The regimes that count planned load differ most in timeliness, verification and public access, and the same requests are 20,677~MW under California's and Texas's counting rules, 5,086~MW under PJM's and the Texas forecast rule, about 2,400~MW at ERCOT's realized rates and about 1,000~MW as measured load (RQ3). The existing system holds 3.8~GW of headroom at 0.5 percent curtailment, enough for the probability-weighted data center load twice over and for half of the weighted peak gap, but for less than a fifth of the upper bound (RQ4). The method is the result: a forecast that can be rebuilt, a supply side discounted the same way, and a gap with its uncertainty and its drivers named.
