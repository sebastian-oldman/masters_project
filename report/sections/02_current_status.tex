\chapter{Current Status of Data Centers and the Grid in California}
\label{ch:current}

This chapter establishes the baseline that the gap analysis in later chapters is measured against. It answers two questions: what the CAISO grid looks like hour by hour today, and how much electricity California's existing data centers already use. Every number is computed from the raw files listed in \texttt{data/raw/manifest.csv}; the notebook \texttt{notebooks/01\_baseline\_grid.ipynb} reproduces each table and figure.

\section{Grid baseline: CAISO demand, net load and imports, 2019--2025}
\label{sec:baseline}

Hourly demand, generation by fuel and net interchange for the CAISO balancing authority come from the EIA-930 Hourly Electric Grid Monitor, using EIA's ``Adjusted'' series \citep{eia930}. Net load is demand minus utility-scale solar and wind. Two definitional points matter. First, EIA-930 demand for CISO includes battery charging and pumping load, whereas CAISO's own reported demand excludes charging; in 2025 the hourly gap between the two series tracks battery charging almost one for one (slope 0.91, correlation 0.87). Both definitions are kept in the processed table. Second, EIA's series contains reporting artifacts, chiefly in early 2019, where demand falls implausibly below CAISO's demand plus charging; those hours are nulled rather than corrected, leaving 8,435 valid hours in 2019 (325 missing, 3.7 percent) and at most 27 missing hours in any later year; annual energy is the mean of the valid hours times the calendar hours, which imputes the missing hours at the annual mean, and the duration curves and profiles below are drawn over valid hours only. Third, EIA-930 carries no CISO interchange at all, in any of its reported, imputed or adjusted variants, from July~2 to November~3, 2024, and no hydro for parts of 2019 and 2020; those hours are filled from CAISO's own five-minute imports and hydro series through a per-year linear calibration fitted on the overlapping hours (correlation 0.986 or better; the fill log is \texttt{ch1\_eia930\_fill\_log.csv}). Fourth, EIA warns that CAISO changed its data collection for natural gas in December 2023 and that the EIA-930 gas series is not comparable across that date \citep{eia_caiso_gas2025}. The warning is confirmed here: through November 2023 EIA's CISO gas generation matches CAISO's own five-minute fuel mix to within 0.1~TWh a month, and from December 2023 it exceeds it by 0.7 to 1.8~TWh a month, 20~TWh in 2024 and 25~TWh in 2025 (\texttt{ch1\_gas\_series\_comparability\_monthly.csv}). Gas figures in Tables~\ref{tab:annual} and \ref{tab:top100} are EIA's series and must not be compared across that month; Table~\ref{tab:gascomp} shows the month-by-month comparison.

Table~\ref{tab:annual} shows a grid whose annual demand has been flat since 2019 while its composition changed. Demand ranged between 216 and 224~TWh in every year. Utility-scale solar output rose from 27.7~TWh to 49.7~TWh and the combined solar and wind share of demand went from a fifth to nearly a third. Net imports fell from a quarter of demand in 2019 to 13 percent in 2023 and 14 to 16 percent in 2024 and 2025. Battery charging, negligible in 2019, reached 14~TWh in 2025, and battery discharge peaked above 10~GW (Table~\ref{tab:battery}). The hourly demand record remains 51.1~GW, set on September~6, 2022 at 18:00.

Figures~\ref{fig:ldc} and \ref{fig:nldc} make the contrast visible. The load duration curves are nearly indistinguishable across years. The net-load duration curves diverge at the low end: by 2024 and 2025 the lowest-net-load hours approach zero, and once battery charging, which is itself a response to the surplus, is removed from demand, utility solar and wind alone exceeded end-use demand in 80 hours of 2024 and 205 hours of 2025. This midday surplus is the physical basis for the flexibility question in Chapter~\ref{ch:together}.

Table~\ref{tab:top100} and Figure~\ref{fig:top100} identify the hours that set capacity needs. In every year at least 90 percent of the top 100 net-load hours fall in July through September and at least 78 percent between 17:00 and 21:00. Their median start time moved from 18:00 in 2019 and 2020 to 19:00 in 2021 and 2022 and to 20:00 from 2023 onward, the signature of solar pushing the net peak past sunset and of storage shaving the early evening. Solar contributes between 0.6 and 1.2~GW on average in these hours; gas contributes 17 to 23~GW and imports 4 to 9~GW. The implication for a flat load such as a data center is direct: each megawatt adds one for one to hours that solar does not serve, and the relevant capacity resources are storage, gas and imports.

\label{sec:prices}

Negative day-ahead prices are now routine in Southern California (Table~\ref{tab:prices}, Figures~\ref{fig:pdc} and \ref{fig:neg}). The SCE aggregation point cleared below zero in 1,131 hours of 2024 and 877 hours of 2025, and in 890 hours of 2026 through September~22; SDG\&E's point saw 989 and 748 hours; PG\&E's 190 and 218. On the matched January~1 to September~22 window the SCE point had 1,045 negative hours in 2024, 775 in 2025 and 890 in 2026, 16, 12 and 14 percent of the hours in the window, so 2026 sits between the two complete years rather than above them (\texttt{ch1\_negative\_price\_hours\_windows.csv}). Between 72 and 94 percent of those hours fall between 10:00 and 16:00, concentrated in February through June, and the average price by hour of day drops to \$7--\$30 per MWh at midday against \$50--\$70 in the evening peak. The daily ICE block prices show almost no negative days, which is why the hourly series is required. This is the California-specific fact that Chapter~\ref{ch:together} uses: a data center able to place consumption in those hours faces a zero or negative marginal energy price, and one that cannot adds to the evening hours where prices and net load are highest.

\section{Hourly carbon intensity}
\label{sec:carbon}

CAISO's Today's Outlook reports five-minute CO$_2$ by source, with imports carried at CAISO's default factor and export emissions netted against them \citep{caiso_outlook}. The indicator used here is that accounting total divided by CAISO demand, energy-weighted per year: an accounting rate for the supply serving CAISO demand, not a measured consumption footprint. Three properties of the data are made explicit. First, because exports are netted, net CO$_2$ is negative in export-heavy hours (374 in 2024, 425 in 2025); the headline series keeps those hours at their negative values, and a variant that floors them at zero is shown as a sensitivity (it raises 2025 from 186.3 to 187.7~g/kWh). Second, CAISO publishes a fixed grid of 288 five-minute rows for every calendar day, including the two daylight-saving transition days, so the repeated autumn hour and the skipped spring hour cannot be separated; an hour is counted as valid when at least six of its twelve intervals exist for both CO$_2$ and demand, hours with no source data are left missing rather than counted as zero, and coverage is 99.8 percent of calendar hours or better in every year. Third, EIA's warning that CAISO's natural gas data collection changed in December 2023 \citep{eia_caiso_gas2025} applies to the EIA-930 gas series, not to CAISO's own accounting: the emission factor implied by CAISO's gas CO$_2$ over CAISO's gas generation stays between 432 and 452~kg/MWh in every month from June 2023 to June 2024 and between 437 and 458 in every year, and CAISO's gas share of demand moves from 48.8 to 47.8 to 47.9 percent across November, December and January. The EPA eGRID output rate for the CAMX subregion is drawn as a reference with a different boundary: a generation-based rate for the whole subregion with no import factor \citep{egrid2023}.

The accounting intensity fell from 257~g/kWh in 2019 to 186~g/kWh in 2025 (Table~\ref{tab:carbon}, Figure~\ref{fig:carbon}); the floored variant gives 257 and 188. The hourly spread widened: in 2025 the fifth percentile was 1~g/kWh and the ninety-fifth 319~g/kWh, and the average midday hour was about 10~g/kWh in spring and 46~g/kWh in summer against roughly 240 to 315~g/kWh at night. eGRID's 2023 CAMX generation rate of 198~g/kWh is lower than the 2023 accounting figure of 242~g/kWh because it excludes the import factor and covers the whole subregion; it is a single-year reference, not a trend. The decline is consistent with the solar and battery additions of Table~\ref{tab:annual}, but the series remains an accounting construct: a validated consumption-emissions series would need CAISO's greenhouse gas tracking reports as an independent check, which is deferred to the supply chapter. For a flat load the annual factor is the relevant one; for a flexible load the diurnal ratio, about five to one in summer and far larger in spring, is the relevant number, and an average intensity is not a marginal emissions factor for load shifting.

\section{How much of the state's power do data centers use}
\label{sec:dcload}

No agency meters California's data center consumption, and the four items in Table~\ref{tab:dc} and Figure~\ref{fig:dc} are not four comparable estimates of one quantity. They are grouped by what they are: one statewide historical estimate, one assumed conversion of a published peak, one illustrative count-based sensitivity and one utility subset. Shares are computed against EIA-861 California retail sales for the year that matches each item, 239.5~TWh in 2023 and 245.7~TWh in 2024 (Parts~A, C and D, which avoids counting delivery-only sales twice) \citep{eia861}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A. Statewide estimate.} EPRI's 2024 study estimates California data centers consumed 9,331,619~MWh in 2023, 3.70 percent of state consumption on EPRI's denominator, the third-largest state total after Virginia and Texas \citep[pp.~13, 28]{epri2024}; against EIA-861 2023 retail sales it is 3.9 percent. This is the only statewide historical estimate in the set.
\item \textbf{B. Assumed conversion.} The CEC's methodology memo states that ``as of December 2025, approximately 1,000 megawatts (MW) of existing data center peak demand operates in California'' \citep[pp.~2, 18]{cec_dc_memo2026}. Converting a peak into energy needs an annual load factor that no source reports. The CEC's daily load-factor profiles for existing facilities range from 75 to 100 percent of the daily maximum \citep{cec_prelim2025}, and an assumed annual factor of 0.80 to 0.95 turns the 1,000~MW into 7.0 to 8.3~TWh, 2.9 to 3.4 percent of 2024 retail sales. It is a conversion scenario, not a measured total.
\item \textbf{C. Illustrative sensitivity.} The Kollar and Grady inventory places 321 of its 2,484 facility points inside California, 102 of them within 12~km of Santa Clara \citep{kollar2025}, but carries no per-facility power, and the Epoch AI hub snapshot contains no California observation \citep{epoch2026}, which is a limit of its coverage rather than a count of zero. The sensitivity multiplies the count by an assumed annual energy per facility taken from the Silicon Valley Power cluster: 42~GWh, or 55 percent of SVP's 2023 retail sales spread over its 58 data centers, an average load of 4.8~MW, scaled by 0.5, 0.75 and 1.0 because the statewide inventory includes many small facilities. The result, 6.8 to 13.6~TWh or 2.8 to 5.7 percent, uses no peak-demand or utilization factor. It is an assumption set whose representativeness is untested, shown to bound the statewide figure rather than to estimate it.
\item \textbf{D. Utility subset.} The municipal utility of Santa Clara reports 58 data centers, the largest cluster on the West Coast, taking 55 percent of its power use \citep{svp_assembly2026}; its web page states 53 percent \citep{svp_dcpage}, and city officials cited about 60 percent in 2025 \citep{sjspotlight2025}. Applied to SVP's 2023 retail sales of 4.48~TWh (Table~\ref{tab:svp}) \citep{svp_factsheets} (its purchased and generated supply was 4.59~TWh, which is what a peak of 669.2~MW times a 78.3 percent load factor reconstructs), the cluster used 2.4 to 2.7~TWh, about one percent of statewide 2023 sales; the percentages come from different dates than the sales figure. SVP also reports that its best-performing data center customers reach about 67 percent utilization of requested capacity and that observed utilization was about 64 percent in 2023 \citep{svp_irp2025}, the empirical origin of the CEC's 67 percent factor; that ratio relates operating peak to requested capacity and is not an annual load factor.
\end{enumerate}

The statewide estimate and the assumed conversion agree at 7 to 9~TWh, roughly 3 to 4 percent of retail sales, and the sensitivity admits 3 to 6 percent. One municipal utility hosts about a quarter to a third of that. The defensible statement for the rest of this study is that existing data centers used about 3 to 4 percent of California's electricity in 2023 to 2025, that the load is geographically concentrated, and that no metered statewide total exists. Against roughly 1~GW of existing peak, the CEC's December 2025 energization requests located in California total 20,677~MW, or 23,277~MW including the 2,600~MW that VEA reports for Nevada \citep{cec_dc_memo2026}, a ratio of about 21 that is the California analogue of Finland's 27x \citep{manner2026} and the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:growth}.

\section{Institutional context}
\label{sec:institutions}

\paragraph{The January 2026 forecast decision.} The CEC adopted the California Energy Demand 2025--2045 forecast set on January~21, 2026 \citep{cec_ced2025}. The set includes three data center scenarios built from the energization tiers. What the joint agencies then chose to do with those scenarios is the institutional fact that matters. The Single Forecast Set Agreement between the CEC, the CPUC and the CAISO, signed in January 2026 and updated on April~15, 2026, directs that CPUC integrated resource planning and CAISO bulk-system transmission studies for the 2026--2027 cycle ``will continue to use the 2024 IEPR planning forecast,'' that the 2027--2028 cycle will use an IRP portfolio based on the same 2024 forecast, and that system resource adequacy requirements for 2027 ``will use the 2025 IEPR planning forecast without known loads'' \citep[pp.~7--8]{sfsa2026}. Only local transmission reliability studies use the 2025 IEPR local reliability forecast with known loads. The agencies give two reasons: the approach ``manages volatility and promotes stability in electric system resource and infrastructure planning currently underway,'' and it ``allows CEC staff additional time to collect and review historical data, observe the impacts of known loads, data centers, and recent federal policy changes, and refine forecast approaches as needed'' \citep[p.~8]{sfsa2026}. In plain terms, the state now tracks data center energization requests in tiers, converts them into forecast scenarios, and then holds the resulting increment out of the forecast that drives generation procurement and bulk transmission for at least two planning cycles. The 2024 IEPR data center mid case, built from the smaller December 2024 tiers, remains the number in use.

\paragraph{Utility pipeline statements.} PG\&E told the CEC in February 2025 that it had ``a large pipeline of applications to interconnect new data centers'' with about 1.5~GW in final engineering or construction and more than half expected within four years \citep{pge_iepr2025}. In July 2025 it announced a 10~GW pipeline over ten years, of which 17 projects totaling about 1.5~GW were in final engineering for operation between 2026 and 2030, and estimated that each gigawatt of new data center demand could lower other customers' bills by one to two percent \citep{pge_press2025}. Its second-quarter 2026 earnings release describes ``an overall pipeline of over 12 gigawatts'' \citep{pge_8k2026}. PG\&E has, however, filed a request for confidentiality over its project-level data center data in the IEPR docket every month since August 2025 \citep{pge_confidentiality}, so its pipeline is public only in aggregate. Southern California Edison took the opposite path. It reported 80~MW of existing data center demand in February 2025 with 200~MW of near-term and 400~MW of mid-term growth \citep{sce_iepr2025}, and it files a public project-level database. The January 2026 vintage lists 115 entries with 8,299~MW of requested peak: 76~MW under signed agreements, 3,313~MW in applications, 1,772~MW in inquiries, and 3,137~MW already canceled \citep{sce_database2026}. The CEC's own summer 2025 tally put SCE's requests at 5,828~MW and PG\&E's at 11,668~MW \citep{cec_prelim2025}. The canceled share in SCE's file, 38 percent of everything ever requested, is the first direct California evidence on attrition and is used in Chapter~\ref{ch:together}.

\paragraph{CPUC large-load proceedings.} The proposal could not confirm a CPUC large-load tariff docket; the record now can. On April~9, 2026 the CPUC opened Rulemaking 26-04-009 on Advanced Electric Rate Design, which states that ``in coordination with the Senate Bill 57 assessment, this proceeding will consider rate design issues for data centers and other large load customers'' \citep[p.~1]{cpuc_oir2026}. Senate Bill 57 (Padilla), chaptered on October~11, 2025, requires the CPUC to assess whether costs of serving new data center loads shift to other customers and to report to the Legislature by January~1, 2027 \citep{sb57}. Senate Bill 886 (Padilla), the California Technology Innovation and Ratepayer Protection Act, was chaptered on September~21, 2026 and requires the CPUC, on or before January~1, 2028, to establish new tariffs or update electric rules for the interconnection and service of participating large customers, and to ensure those tariffs ``prevent the creation of stranded costs for, or cost shifts to, nonparticipating customers'' \citep{sb886,kqed2026}. Assembly Bill 222, which would have required data center energy reporting and efficiency metrics, was held in the Senate Appropriations Committee on August~29, 2025 \citep{ab222}. On the interconnection side, the CPUC approved an interim PG\&E Electric Rule~30 on July~24, 2025 to streamline transmission-level service for large customers that fund the required infrastructure up front \citep{cpuc_rule30}. The sequence is consistent: the state has moved from studying the cost question (SB~57) to mandating a tariff answer (SB~886) within a year, while the forecast that drives procurement still excludes the load the tariff is meant to price.
