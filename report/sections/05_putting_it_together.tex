\chapter{Putting It Together}
\label{ch:together}

This chapter answers RQ2, RQ4 and RQ3 with the pieces built so far. Section~\ref{sec:demand} turns the December 2025 tiers of Chapter~\ref{ch:growth} into 2030 demand under four counting rules, on top of the IEPR's own low, mid and high forecast of everything that is not a data center. Section~\ref{sec:mc} draws 10,000 joint scenarios of that demand and of the probability-weighted supply of Chapter~\ref{ch:supply} and reports the distribution of the 2030 gap in energy and at the net peak, with the proposal's upper bound carried in every table. Section~\ref{sec:sens} ranks the inputs by one-at-a-time swings and Sobol indices. Section~\ref{sec:headroom} replicates the Duke Nicholas Institute's curtailment-enabled headroom on CAISO hourly load and sets it against the gap (RQ4). Section~\ref{sec:regimes} compares the six data and measurement regimes and restates California's headline number under each of their counting rules (RQ3), and Section~\ref{sec:emissions} adds the emissions of a flat against a curtailable load. Code is in \texttt{src/ch4\_gap.py}, \texttt{src/ch4\_regimes.py}, \texttt{scripts/run\_chapter4.py} and \texttt{notebooks/04\_gap\_model.ipynb} and \texttt{05\_regimes\_flexibility.ipynb}; \texttt{make ch4} rebuilds everything in about a minute.

\paragraph{The tiers and the non-data-center baseline.} The starting point is the CEC's December 2025 request data: 5,086~MW with signed agreements, 9,587~MW in active applications and 8,604~MW of inquiries statewide, of which the 2,600~MW application reported by Valley Electric Association sits in Nevada, so the California-only total is 20,677~MW (Table~\ref{tab:ch4tiers}). The three cases are labelled low (Planning), mid (Baseline) and high (Local Reliability); the fourth, which adds the utilities' known loads through a separate adjustment line, is shown for reference. \paragraph{The ERCOT-calibrated stock-flow case.} ERCOT publishes the stock of large-load requests in each phase of its interconnection process, which makes it possible to estimate how fast projects actually move from one phase to the next. Monthly totals from January 2025 to March 2026, the monthly stocks of projects with approved planning studies and with approval to energize from May 2025 to March 2026, and six snapshots of the queue by status between March and July 2026 were transcribed from ERCOT's newsletters, its March 2026 TAC report, its December 2025 and June 2026 board decks, its April 2026 legislative decks and its April, June, July and August 2026 operational overviews (Figure~\ref{fig:ercot}, Section~\ref{sec:regimes}) \citep{ercot_tac2026,ercot_monthly2025,ercot_board2026,ercot_ops2026}. Approvals to energize reached 9,456~MW by July 2026, of which 4,370~MW was observed operating that month and 4,316~MW in August. From these, four monthly hazards are estimated (Table~\ref{tab:ercotrates}): 2.1 percent a month from no studies submitted to under review, an upper bound because cancellations leave the same stock; 0.75 percent from under review to studies approved; 1.6 percent from studies approved to approval to energize; and 3.1 percent from approval to energize to observed energization. A Markov chain with those hazards gives the probability that a project starting in each phase is energized within 60 months, the distance from the December 2025 vintage to December 2030: 37 percent for a project whose studies are approved, 6 percent for one under review and 2 percent for one without studies (Table~\ref{tab:ercotchain}). Mapping inquiries to no studies submitted, applications to under review and signed agreements to approved studies, and applying the CEC's utilization factor, gives 1,622~MW and 12.5~TWh in 2030, almost the same as the CEC central case. Doubling every hazard, which is what ERCOT's move to a batch process is meant to achieve, raises the three probabilities to 72, 25 and 12 percent; a 0.5 percent monthly cancellation hazard lowers them slightly. The rates were measured while ERCOT's queue quadrupled and its studies fell behind, so they describe study throughput as much as project viability, which is why the Monte Carlo treats the tier probabilities as uncertain over the CEC and ERCOT values rather than fixing them.

\section{The Monte Carlo gap model}
\label{sec:mc}

\paragraph{Definitions.} The energy gap in 2030 is the growth of demand from 2025 to 2030, non-data-center growth in the drawn IEPR case plus the data center energy of the drawn case, minus the growth of supply, the energy of the planned units that complete at recent capacity factors, minus the energy of the units that retire, plus the change in net imports. The net-peak gap is the growth of the statewide coincident peak times one plus the planning reserve margin, minus the change in ELCC-weighted capacity. Both are increments, so the accounting boundaries of the CEC forecast and of the EIA generation data cancel, and a positive value means that demand grows faster than the resources the weighted pipeline delivers at recent operating patterns; it has to be met by running the existing fleet harder, by importing more, or by resources not yet in the planned list. The absolute balances, which compare 2030 levels directly, are reported alongside; they use in-state ELCC only and so leave out the peak contribution of imports, which in 2025 sat on top of an in-state surplus of about 1.5~GW over the statewide peak with a 16 percent margin.

\paragraph{Inputs.} Table~\ref{tab:mcinputs} lists the thirteen inputs and the evidence behind their ranges: the tier probabilities span the CEC's Planning and Local Reliability confidence levels with lower bounds near the ERCOT chain; utilization runs from ERCOT's observed 46 percent (the August 2026 monthly peak of approved loads over the approved total) to PJM's 70 percent with the CEC's 67 percent as the mode; the share of realized capacity on line by 2030 spans the CEC's own ramp profile (36 and 60 percent by 2030) and PJM's 36-month rule; the load factor is the range of Chapter~\ref{ch:current}; each of the 229 planned generating units completes with its Chapter~\ref{ch:supply} probability; the hydro capacity factor spans 2010--2025; solar and wind output moves within 10 percent; 2030 net imports span the CEC's 2012--2024 statewide series; the IEPR case is drawn with equal weights; Diablo Canyon operates or retires with equal probability, because the Nuclear Regulatory Commission renewed both licences on April 2, 2026 while SB~846 authorises operation only through 2030 and any extension needs an act of the Legislature \citep{gov_ca_diablo2026,nrc_diablo2026}; and the reserve margin is uniform between 15 and 17 percent.

\paragraph{Results.} The median 2030 energy gap is 50~TWh, with a 5th to 95th percentile range of 19 to 78~TWh, and the median net-peak gap is 7.2~GW (4.2 to 10.1~GW); both are positive in every draw (Table~\ref{tab:mcsummary}, Figure~\ref{fig:gapdist}). The data center contribution is 2.3~GW and 18~TWh at the median and 1.4 to 3.6~GW across the 5th to 95th percentiles. The gap is dominated by what is not a data center: non-data-center growth alone is 24 to 45~TWh and 3.6 to 5.8~GW, while the weighted pipeline adds on average only 5~TWh and 1.1~GW of ELCC once retirements are netted, and it removes 18~TWh and 2.2~GW in the half of the draws where Diablo Canyon retires (median gaps of 59~TWh and 8.4~GW with it retired against 42~TWh and 6.2~GW with it operating). Closing the median energy gap with the existing gas fleet would require raising its capacity factor from the 0.24 of 2023--2025 to 0.40, and the 95th percentile to 0.49. The proposal's upper bound, every requested megawatt operating flat in 2030 with the central supply, gives an energy gap of 202 to 242~TWh and a peak gap of 25.5 to 30.2~GW across the three IEPR cases and both Diablo Canyon settings, four to five times the probability-weighted 95th percentile; those rows appear in every results table.

\section{Headroom from load flexibility (RQ4)}
\label{sec:headroom}

\paragraph{Method.} The Duke Nicholas Institute asked how much constant new load a balancing authority could absorb without exceeding the demand its planners already serve, provided the new load accepts a small amount of curtailment \citep{duke2025}. Their method is replicated on the cleaned CAISO hourly demand of Chapter~\ref{ch:current} for 2019--2025: a constant load L is added to every hour, the excess of demand plus L over the seasonal peak threshold is counted as curtailment, the thresholds are the maximum winter and non-winter demand across all years with winter defined as November to February for Pacific-coast balancing authorities, the annual curtailment rate is averaged over the years, and L is solved for curtailment limits of 0.25, 0.5, 1 and 5 percent. Missing hours are interpolated as in Duke's cleaning. Three variants test the definition: a December-to-February winter, the 2022--2025 subsample, and a single annual threshold. The headroom is also solved year by year for 2019 to 2025 under two criteria: Duke's, curtailed energy under 0.25, 0.5, 1 or 5 percent of the load's potential energy, and the stricter one in the phase plan, the hours in which demand plus the addition exceeds the historical peak threshold kept under the same shares of the year's hours, which has a closed form from the sorted margins to the threshold.

\paragraph{Results.} The headroom is 3.1~GW at 0.25 percent curtailment, 3.8~GW at 0.5 percent, 4.6~GW at 1 percent and 8.0~GW at 5 percent (Table~\ref{tab:headroom}, Figure~\ref{fig:headroom}), against Duke's 4.2, 5.0 and 5.9~GW for CAISO on 2016--2024 data. The shortfall against Duke comes from the sample of years and from the winter threshold, 31,226~MW here: 97 to 99 percent of the curtailment falls in November to February, when winter evening demand sits close to the winter peak, and the 2022--2025 subsample gives 2.7 to 4.1~GW. With a single annual threshold of 51,104~MW the headroom would be 11.6 to 16.0~GW, which shows how much of the answer is a definition of season. At the 0.5 percent headroom the new load is curtailed in about 197 hours a year, in 134 of which less than 90 percent of it is available and in 18 less than half, and in the worst hour of the seven years the whole 3.8~GW is cut (Table~\ref{tab:headroomdetail}). Solved year by year (Table~\ref{tab:headroomyear}), the energy criterion gives 3.3 to 4.2~GW at 0.5 percent, 2.4 to 3.7~GW at 0.25 percent and 4.2 to 4.9~GW at 1 percent, lowest in 2025 and highest in 2023 and 2024; the hours criterion is stricter, 2.0 to 2.9~GW at 0.5 percent and 2.6 to 3.3~GW at 1 percent, because it charges a full hour for any excess however small. With a single historical peak instead of seasonal thresholds the same criteria give 7.0 to 11.7~GW and 11.4 to 16.2~GW at 0.5 percent (Table~\ref{tab:headroomyearsingle}).

\paragraph{Against the gap.} The 0.5 percent headroom is 2.4 times the CEC's central data center addition and 2.3 times the ERCOT-calibrated one, about equal to the PJM firm-only case, 92 percent of the CEC's high case, and 18 percent of the upper bound (Table~\ref{tab:headroomgap}). Set against the Monte Carlo net-peak gap, which includes everything else that grows, it covers 53 percent of the median and 38 percent of the 95th percentile, and 13 percent of the upper-bound peak gap; the 1 percent headroom covers 65 percent of the median gap. Flexibility is therefore enough to place the probability-weighted data center load, and about half of the whole probability-weighted peak gap, without new capacity, but not the upper bound. On the energy side the argument is stronger (Table~\ref{tab:energyside}): the SCE load-aggregation point had 877 hours of negative day-ahead prices in 2025 and 1,131 in 2024, 78 percent of them between 10 and 16 h (77 to 94 percent across the three load-aggregation points), and CAISO curtailed 3.8~TWh of wind and solar in 2025 and 4.9~TWh in January to August 2026 alone, the equivalent of a 430~MW load running flat all year, so a load that can follow the midday surplus adds energy the system is already discarding.

\section{Data and measurement regimes (RQ3)}
\label{sec:regimes}

\paragraph{Crosswalk.} Table~\ref{tab:crosswalk} compares six regimes on granularity, stage taxonomy, verification, timeliness, coverage and public access: the CEC's energization tiers \citep{cec_dc_memo2026,cec_tn272026,sce_database2026}, ERCOT's large-load interconnection reporting \citep{ercot_tac2026,ercot_ops2026}, PJM's load forecast adjustments \citep{pjm_lar2025,pjm_loadreport2026}, EIA's pilot data center surveys \citep{eia_pilot2026}, Texas SB~6 with the PUCT rules that implement it \citep{texas_sb6,ercot_house2026}, and FERC Docket RM26-4 \citep{ferc_rm26_4,caiso_rm26_4,nerc_llplan2026,ferc_showcause2026}. Only ERCOT reports monthly and by phase; only PJM publishes a firm and non-firm split with the discounts it applies; only EIA measures consumption rather than requests, and it has not yet published; Texas and the FERC docket set thresholds (75 and 20~MW) and verification duties (fees, security, disclosure) rather than data; and California's tiers have no size threshold, no public deposit or site-control test, and project-level detail only where SCE chose to publish it. Table~\ref{tab:scores} and Figure~\ref{fig:scores} score each cell from 0 to 3 under a stated rubric: ERCOT scores highest (13 of 18) on the strength of its monthly, phase-by-phase reporting, the CEC tiers 10 with full coverage but weak verification and annual timing, PJM 8, Texas SB~6 and the FERC docket 7 each because they set duties rather than publish data, and the EIA pilot 4 because it is facility-level but unpublished and one-off. Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} maps the five stages that every regime distinguishes in some form, and Figure~\ref{fig:ercot} draws the ERCOT time series that the stock-flow case was estimated from: 63~GW of tracked requests in December 2024, 239~GW in March 2026 before that month's submissions, 411~GW two weeks later and 467~GW by July 2026, against approvals to energize that rose from 6.9 to 9.5~GW and observed load that stayed near 4~GW. PJM's Table B-9 adds 33.7~GW of large-load adjustments above the embedded load to its 2030 summer peak (38.8~GW of total load associated with adjustments) against requests of roughly 60~GW in its preliminary November 2025 summary, of which about 35~GW were firm (Table~\ref{tab:pjm}).

\paragraph{California's headline number under each rule.} Table~\ref{tab:headline} restates the same requests under each regime's counting rule. The CEC and ERCOT both count every request regardless of study status, so the headline stays at 20,677~MW (23,277 with VEA). The Texas forecast rule that admits only executed interconnection agreements after 2026 and the PJM firm-only rule both give 5,086~MW, or 3,560~MW of demand at PJM's utilization; ERCOT's realized phase-transition rates would energize about 2,400~MW by 2030; and a consumption survey of the EIA type would report the roughly 1,000~MW that exists. Size thresholds cannot be applied to the CEC aggregates, but in SCE's project-level database 76 percent of active requested megawatts are in projects of 75~MW or more and 95 percent in projects of 20~MW or more (Table~\ref{tab:scesizes}), so the Texas and FERC thresholds would leave most of the capacity in scope while removing most of the projects.

\section{Emissions of a flat against a curtailable load}
\label{sec:emissions}

The CAISO hourly accounting intensity of Chapter~\ref{ch:current} gives the emissions of a 1~MW load that is flat, curtailed to zero in the highest-intensity 5, 10 or 25 percent of hours, or shifted, with the same energy moved from those hours into the lowest-intensity hours (Table~\ref{tab:emissions}, Figure~\ref{fig:emissions}). A flat load carried 183~t of CO$_2$ per GWh in 2025, down from 239 in 2023. Curtailing the highest-intensity quarter of hours lowers the intensity of what the load still consumes by 20 percent; shifting that energy into the lowest-intensity quarter, whose mean accounting intensity was 44~g/kWh in 2025, lowers it by 34 percent to 121~t per GWh. Flexibility therefore buys both the headroom of Section~\ref{sec:headroom} and a third of the load's emissions.

\section{What the chapter answers}
\label{sec:ch4answers}

RQ2: with the CEC's tiers weighted by realization, utilization and ramp and the supply side weighted by California's own completion record, the 2030 gap is 50~TWh (19 to 78) and 7.2~GW (4.2 to 10.1) at the net peak, of which data centers are 18~TWh and 2.3~GW at the median; the rest is ordinary load growth and the retirements and slow additions of the supply side. The proposal's upper bound of 202 to 242~TWh and 25 to 30~GW is four to five times the weighted 95th percentile. RQ4: curtailment-enabled headroom of 3.8~GW at 0.5 percent (3.3 to 4.2~GW year by year, or 2.0 to 2.9~GW if every hour above the peak counts) is enough to place the probability-weighted data center load two times over and half of the whole weighted peak gap, but 18 percent of the upper bound, and the surplus midday energy that a flexible load could use is real and growing. RQ3: the same requests are 20,677~MW under the CEC's and ERCOT's rules, 5,086~MW under the PJM and Texas forecast rules, about 2,400~MW at ERCOT's realized transition rates and about 1,000~MW of measured load; the regimes differ most in timeliness (monthly in ERCOT, annual elsewhere), verification (deposits and agreements in Texas and PJM, none in California's public record) and public access (aggregates everywhere, project-level detail only from SCE).
